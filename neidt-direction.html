01 / Diagnosis

The site isn't dated.
It's underpriced.

Jeff's instinct is right, and his own note names it: the site "leans slightly too hard on the word writer, which inadvertently downcharges your strategic value." That's the whole brief. Everything else is symptom.

Symptom

"I'm (not just) a writer." The headline defines him by what he isn't. Negative constructions never sell up — they read as apology.

Symptom

"Concepts + Copy." Both words are deliverables. A CD buying deliverables pays a day rate. A CMO buying judgment pays a retainer.

Symptom

130 client names in a wall. Target next to Pronto Pups. Volume without hierarchy reads as "available," not "selective."

Symptom

Blog: 3 posts, last one Aug 2020. A visibly abandoned Insights section is worse than none — it dates the whole site to six years ago.

Symptom

Work sorted by client, tagged by format. "Elkay — Advertising." That's a vendor's filing system. It tells a buyer nothing about what problem got solved.

Symptom

Six nav items, one of which is a list. Home / About / Clients / Work / Insights / Contact. Clients isn't a destination, it's a proof point in the wrong place.

The buried lede

3M's first global brand campaign in 25 years — and it's a tile in a grid.

Jeff worked on Science Applied to Life alongside Wolff Olins, BBDO New York and BBDO Minneapolis. That single credit answers the question his notes keep circling — "how do I show larger-scale solutions?" — better than any Caper Method section ever could. Right now it's a thumbnail labeled "3M Brand — Branding," visually equal to a neighborhood brewery.

What's already working — keep it
  • The voice. "I'm shorter than you'd imagine," the Whitman lift, the oxford comma bit. It's specific and it's his. We're not sanding that off — we're aiming it higher.
  • The client roster is genuinely deep. 3M, Target, Google, Microsoft, Optum, Chobani, Colgate, Hilton, Cargill, US Army. The problem is presentation, not substance.
  • Agency-side relationships (BBDO, Wolff Olins, Fast Horse, KNOCK, Mythic, Publicis) — that's a second, separate business and it deserves its own line.
  • "Craft clients." Buried on the About page. It's a real filter and it's well put.
02 / Positioning

Jeff already wrote it.
It's in his notes.

From the brief, under "Thoughts"

"Value is the art of noticing, making connections."

That line does three jobs at once, which is why it should be the spine of the site:

1. It's the strategy

Positioning is noticing — finding the true thing a company can't see because they're inside it. "The average person uses 3M technology 100 times a day" is a noticing, not a copy line.

2. It rescues "writer"

It reframes writing as the evidence of strategic thinking rather than the service. Writers notice for a living. That's a credential, not a limitation.

3. It saves the domain

No rebrand needed. neidtwriter.com stops being a ceiling the moment the site argues that the writing is how the strategy gets proven.

Positioning statement

Jeff Neidt is a brand strategist and creative director who builds foundations — positioning, architecture, platforms — and then proves them in language precise enough to survive contact with a 60,000-product company.

Said shorter, for a bio line: Brand strategy and creative direction, with a writer's ear for the difference between true and almost true.

Homepage headline — three territories

All three keep the voice. They differ in how hard they push the reposition. My recommendation is A.

A
Most brand problems
are noticing problems.

Sub: I'm Jeff Neidt. I find the true thing your company can't see anymore — then write it down in a way people repeat.

Leads with a strategic claim, not a service. Sets up every case study to pay it off. Owns a word nobody else in this category owns.

Recommended
B
A brand is a sentence
somebody has to repeat.

Sub: Most of them aren't written well. That's usually a strategy problem wearing a copy problem's clothes.

The most elegant argument for why a writer should lead strategy. Slightly narrower — frames everything as verbal, undersells the creative direction.

Twenty years of
paying attention.

Sub: Brand strategy, creative direction, and the writing that holds both together.

Safest. Credential-forward, reads senior immediately, least distinctive. The one to pick if Jeff gets cold feet.

Safe

Four values — the Distill move, made his

Jeff flagged Distill Health's four values ("Be smart / Be real / Be diligent / Be curious"). Same structural device, but built off noticing so it reinforces the positioning instead of floating free. These live on About.

01 — Notice first

The brief is never the problem. The problem is the thing nobody in the room said out loud yet.

02 — Say it plain

If it needs a glossary, it isn't finished. Clarity isn't the opposite of craft — it's the proof of it.

03 — Make the connection

Good ideas are usually two known things nobody had put next to each other. That's the job.

04 — Leave it better

Good work makes somebody feel something — confident, assured, seen. If nobody feels anything, it was just output.

Value 04 is lifted almost directly from his note: "Thoughts about what's good work: Make somebody feel a certain way. Feel good. Or confident. Or assured."

Why drop "Project"

"Non-Billable Project" is three words in a four-item nav — it wobbles the line and reads as one specific thing. "Non-Billable" is one word, slightly mysterious, and unmistakably a point of view. A 130-name list isn't a destination — nobody navigates to it. It becomes two things instead: a logo/name marquee on the homepage (the recognizable 20), and a full expandable roster at the bottom of Work for the people who want to scan it.

The logo goes home. Every site works this way now; the nav item is a 2012 artifact and it's costing a slot.

04 / Work

Sort by the problem,
not by the client.

Jeff asked: 12 samples, or 3 and a link? Neither. The count isn't the issue — the taxonomy is. Right now work is filed by client and tagged by deliverable ("Elkay — Advertising"), which is how a vendor organizes a portfolio. Three categories, ordered deliberately, do the repositioning silently:

01 — Foundations

Brand platforms, positioning, naming, architecture, messaging systems. The highest-value work, listed first, so it's the first thing a buyer sees.

3M · Microsoft ExpertZone · Hyde Brewing · Velera* · Vetter* · LTD Brewing

02 — Campaigns

Go-to-market, integrated, advertising. The proof he can carry a platform all the way to market.

Optum · Petco · Northstar · Elkay · Riverlights* · Invisalign* · UHC* · Ball Park Cafe

03 — Content & Film

Long-form, editorial, scripted video. Where the volume and the range live.

3M Content (200+ stories) · SportsEngine · LiftOne* · Dentsply* · NBC Sports* · Rotten Tomatoes*

* Needs Jeff to confirm role, year and assets before it goes live — see §08.

Answering "how do I show larger-scale solutions?"

Not with a Caper Method section — with a collaborator line. "With Wolff Olins, BBDO New York and BBDO Minneapolis" under the 3M case study does in eight words what a paragraph about a creative collective can't. It proves he operates at that altitude without him having to claim it. Recommendation: retire Caper Method as a section; keep it as a credit where relevant.

Case study template — five fields, one of them is the whole strategy

01 — The situation

Two sentences. What the company faced. No throat-clearing, no "in today's crowded marketplace."

02 — What I noticed

One sentence, set large, in the display face. The insight that unlocked it. This field is the entire positioning, repeated 14 times across the site. 3M's is already written and it's perfect: "The average person touches 3M technology 100 times a day and can't name the company once."

03 — What we made

Deliverables, plainly listed. Scale lives here: "brand platform copy across five business divisions, a brand training hub, and 200+ newsroom stories in ten months."

04 — What happened

Result, attributed where it's the agency's or client's number. "ChappellRoberts reports the campaign beat sales goals by 160%" — never stated flat. Where there's no metric, say what shipped and where it ran. Omitting the section entirely is fine; faking it isn't.

05 — Credits

Role · Year · Agency · Collaborators. Mono type, small, factual. This is the seniority signal.

The clients problem

Jeff asked how to repurpose the list. Three moves:

Marquee, not wall

Homepage: a slow horizontal ticker of ~20 recognizable names in the display face — 3M, Target, Google, Microsoft, Optum, Chobani, Colgate-Palmolive, Hilton, Cargill, General Mills, Petco, US Army. Names, not logos: cheaper, cleaner, no permissions problem, and it stays on-brand as a typographic site.

Split the audiences

Two roster columns at the bottom of Work: Brands and Agencies. He sells to both and they buy for different reasons. The agency list (BBDO, Wolff Olins, Publicis, Fast Horse, KNOCK, Mythic) is a credential the brand list can't carry.

The returning-client stat

His best line and it's nowhere on the site. Put a real number on the homepage: "Most of my clients hire me more than once." Better if he'll count it — "X of my last Y engagements were repeat clients." That single fact outperforms 130 names.

05 / Visual system

Quiet paper.
Hard lines.
One loud color.

Jeff's references pull two ways: Riot Fest, Waterparks and Brutalist Brewing are loud and graphic; selfaware.studio and the Cargo templates are warm and quiet — and he says selfaware "is probably closer to what I am." He's right, but the loud references aren't noise, they're the personality he doesn't want to lose. So: an editorial base that can go loud on command. Warm paper and serif body text for the enterprise buyer at Optum. Hairline rules, mono labels and a vermilion accent for the personality. And a fully inverted Non-Billable section where the volume goes all the way up.

Why vermilion and not the obvious acid green. Riot Fest's electric palette goes muddy against warm paper — high-chroma greens and cyans fight cream. Vermilion (#F03C13) sits in the same family as ink and printing, holds its saturation on a warm ground, reads confident rather than gimmicky, and passes contrast on both paper and ink. It's used at roughly 3% coverage: labels, one word in the headline, hover states, and the Non-Billable room. Restraint is what makes it loud.

A grotesk with deliberate wonky details — variable weight and optical size, free on Google Fonts. Reads professional at a glance and slightly odd on inspection, which is exactly the brief: personality that doesn't spook a healthcare CMO. Used for H1–H3, work titles, and nav.

Variable, free, warm, designed for screen. Used for all running text, leads, and case study prose.

Utility

The brutalist device, applied with a scalpel. Every label, credit and category is mono uppercase at 11–12px with wide tracking. It's what makes the layout read as an index or a spec sheet rather than a portfolio — and it's doing the "use of design and lines" job from his Brutalist Brewing reference.

Scale

H1 clamp(44px, 8.5vw, 116px) / -0.045em · H2 clamp(30px, 4.6vw, 60px) · H3 clamp(21px, 2.4vw, 30px) · Body 18px/1.55 · Lead clamp(20px, 2.3vw, 27px) weight 300 · Label 11px/0.14em. Measure caps at 70ch for prose, 34ch for leads.

Structure & devices

The hairline

1px ink rules separate everything; 3px rules separate chapters. No shadows, no rounded corners, no cards floating on grey. Borders do all the containing. This is the Brutalist Brewing lesson applied to a warm palette.

The mono gutter

A 150px left column carries a sticky mono section label while content scrolls past it on the right. Cheap to build, instantly distinctive, and it makes long pages navigable. It's the layout device you're reading right now.

Ledger rows

Work, insights and the noticed list are all rows with mono metadata, not image cards. Scales to any count, loads instantly, and puts the writing first — appropriate for a writer.

The invert

Full-bleed ink sections with vermilion accents mark the personality moments. Used sparingly: the Non-Billable page, one homepage band, the footer. The contrast is the point — it only reads loud because the rest is quiet.

Grid cells

1px-gap grids over an ink background produce hairline-divided modules with no extra markup. Used for values, categories, FAQ. See the four-value block in §02.

Images, finally

The current site is starved of imagery. Work needs real stills. Recommendation: consistent treatment — full-bleed, warm-toned, no drop shadows, no laptop mockups. And one honest portrait of Jeff, not a headshot.

Motion

  • Rules draw in. Hairlines animate scaleX 0→1 on scroll entry, 400ms, staggered. Subtle, and it makes the structure feel drawn rather than placed.
  • Rows invert on hover. Background fills vermilion, type flips to paper, row insets 12px. 180ms. This is the one piece of delight and it's on the most-used element.
  • Headlines reveal by line on first paint only. Never on scroll — text that animates in every time is an accessibility problem and it gets old by page three.
  • No parallax, no scroll-jacking, no cursor followers. They're the tell of a site designed to impress designers. His buyers are CMOs and agency ECDs.
  • prefers-reduced-motion: reduce disables everything except opacity. Non-negotiable.

07 / Non-Billable

The room where
the volume
goes up.

This is the loud moment the hybrid direction was built for. Inverted ground, vermilion accents, bigger type, looser rules. It's also where three of Jeff's open questions get solved at once — the dead blog, the "17 random things" personality problem, and the LinkedIn tie-in.

The blog problem, stated honestly

Three posts. Last one August 2020. Jeff is not going to start blogging — nobody with a full client load does, and pretending otherwise just puts another "last updated 2020" timestamp on the site in eighteen months. So don't build a blog. Build a habit that's small enough to actually keep.

Things I've Noticed

Jeff flagged Gari Cruze's 17 Random Things — great page, and the instinct behind it is right: buyers hire people, not portfolios. But copying the format (and the number) is a tell, and "random" is the one word we can't use on a site arguing that his value is noticing.

So: a running numbered list with no fixed count. Observations, half of them about brands and half about his kids or a parking lot or a badly worded sign. It's the personality page, the proof of the positioning, and the content engine — all one object.

It proves the thesis

A site that claims "value is the art of noticing" and then shows 60 things he's noticed isn't asserting a positioning. It's demonstrating it. Nothing else on the site works this hard.

It's low enough friction to survive

Two sentences. No headline, no hero image, no editing pass. He can add one from his phone. A blog post is a project; a noticing is a thought.

It solves the LinkedIn tie-in

Publish to the site first, then post the same text to LinkedIn with a link back. The site becomes the archive and the canonical source; LinkedIn is distribution. It also means every LinkedIn post drives traffic to a page that sells him.

Format on the page — mono index number, one or two sentences, date. Ledger rows again, but looser:

Patron & Saint

From his note: "never heard of it, have you? That's because it doesn't exist. But it probably should have." A brand he invented that has no company attached to it. This is the best idea in the entire brief and it should be a full page, not a footnote.

Give it the complete treatment — logo, positioning line, a few executions, and the joke played entirely straight. A speculative brand, built properly, is a portfolio piece that doubles as an argument: this is what I do when nobody is paying me and nobody is saying no. It shows range, taste and point of view in a way no approved client work can, because there's no client fingerprint on it. And per his note — sell the shirt. A physical object makes the joke real and it's the kind of thing people mention to other people.

What else lives here
  • The three existing posts — keep the 2020 virtual-teams piece if he still stands behind it, archive the 2015 ones. No visible dates on the index, so the archive doesn't advertise its own age.
  • Longer pieces — a slot for real essays, published when he has one and never on a schedule. Two good ones beat twelve obligatory ones.
  • Poetry, if he's willing. He was a poetry instructor and teaches at The Loft. The current site mentions it once and moves on quickly. On a site about noticing, it's the most credible evidence he's got — and it's the thing no other brand strategist can put on the page.

Things to be careful about

  • Attribute other people's numbers. ChappellRoberts claims Riverlights beat sales goals by 160% — that's their figure on their case study. Write "ChappellRoberts reports…" not "we drove…".

  • Check the client roster before republishing it. It's roughly 130 brands and 55 agencies, some going back a decade. A few may have been acquired, renamed or folded. Worth one pass.
  • The 3M campaign is externally verifiable — Forbes covered the March 2015 launch. That's a link worth having, and it's the strongest third-party proof on the site.
09 / Build

How it gets made.

Custom static site — hand-written HTML/CSS/JS, no framework, no build step required. Hosted on Netlify or Vercel, deployed from a Git repo. Fast, permanent, free at this scale, and it will look exactly like the design rather than approximately like it.

Why not Cargo

Jeff linked two Cargo templates for the look, and we've taken that reference. But the ledger rows, the sticky mono gutter and the invert system all fight Cargo's editor. Custom gets the design intact for the same effort.

The editing tradeoff

Static means Jeff can't edit it himself without touching code. Mitigation: markdown files for case studies and the noticed list, compiled at deploy. He edits a text file, pushes, site updates. If he'd rather not, adding Sanity or Decap CMS later is a contained job — worth deciding before we build, not after.

Performance target

Two Google fonts, no framework, no analytics bloat. Sub-1s first paint, 100 Lighthouse on everything but images. A writer's site that loads slowly is an own goal.

Don't skip

Redirects from every old URL (/clients, /insights, /work/*) so a decade of links and search equity survives. Semantic HTML, real focus states, alt text, reduced-motion. Sitemap and OG images per page — his work gets shared on LinkedIn.
